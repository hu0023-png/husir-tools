🧮 Rule of 72 & Simple Interest

How fast money doubles · and what simple interest earns along the way

📘 About this tool

The Rule of 72 is the fastest mental shortcut in personal finance: divide 72 by an annual return to estimate how many years it takes money to double when interest compounds. At 8%, money doubles in about 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years; at 6%, about 12 years. The rule is surprisingly accurate for the 4–12% range where most investments live, and it makes the cost of low returns concrete — a savings account at 0.5% would need 144 years to double, which is why parking long-term money in cash quietly loses buying power.

This page gives you both directions — rate to doubling time, or target years to the required rate — plus the exact compounding answer for comparison and the sobering “simple interest” alternative, where doubling takes 100 ÷ rate years because interest is never reinvested. The second panel is a plain simple-interest calculator: the everyday math of loans, bonds, and short-term savings, where interest is paid only on the original principal. A side-by-side note shows how much compounding adds over the same term.

Everything is calculated locally in your browser — no account, no login, no data uploaded. These are arithmetic tools for understanding, not investment or loan advice: real returns vary, fees apply, and loans come with terms and penalties that no formula box can fully capture.

⚠️ Disclaimer: Estimates for planning only — not tax or financial advice. Rates and rules change, so verify with your state or a qualified professional before filing or making big decisions.

➗ Why 72 and not 70?
72 has many easy divisors (2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12), so mental math stays clean, and it tracks the true math closely across the 4–12% rates people actually use. The mathematically exact figure for continuous compounding is about 69.3, but 72 is friendlier.

💡 Does the Rule of 72 work for simple interest?
No — it assumes compounding. With simple interest, doubling takes 100 ÷ rate years (e.g. 20 years at 5%). This tool shows both numbers side by side so the difference is easy to see.

🧾 Where does simple interest actually appear?
Some personal loans, short-term CDs and bonds, car loans (flat-rate quotes), and most “interest charged per day” situations such as payday-style lending or late fees. Mortgages and most bank savings compound, which is why their math differs.

📈 What return should I test?
Historical long-run US stock returns are often quoted near 7–10% before inflation; high-yield savings have paid roughly 3.5–4.5% APY in recent years. Test both a low and a high rate — doubling time swings a lot between them.

🔁 Is 72 ÷ rate the same as the exact doubling time?
Almost. At 6%, the rule says 12.0 years; the exact answer with annual compounding is 11.9. At 15% the rule drifts (4.8 vs 5.0 exact), so this page shows the exact figure too.

⚠️ Is this financial advice?
No. These are quick planning numbers. Inflation, taxes, and fees reduce real returns, and loan agreements have fine print — verify terms with the lender before borrowing.

📐 Quick formulas
Rule of 72: years to double ≈ 72 ÷ annual rate (rate in %)
Exact doubling years = ln(2) ÷ ln(1 + rate)
Simple-interest doubling years = 100 ÷ rate
Simple interest earned = principal × rate × years
Maturity amount = principal × (1 + rate × years)
