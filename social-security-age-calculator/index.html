🧓 Social Security Full Retirement Age

Born 1937 or later · find your FRA & how claiming at 62–70 changes your check

📘 About this tool

Your Full Retirement Age (FRA) is the age at which Social Security pays you 100% of your Primary Insurance Amount — the benefit shown on your annual statement. For anyone born from 1943 through 1954 it is 66, and it rises in two-month steps for later birth years until it reaches 67 for people born in 1960 or later. This tool looks up your FRA from your birth year and shows exactly how the Social Security Administration's rules change your monthly check if you claim early (as young as 62) or delay it (up to 70).

Claiming before FRA permanently reduces your benefit by 5/9 of 1% for each of the first 36 months and 5/12 of 1% for each additional month. Waiting past FRA adds Delayed Retirement Credits of 2/3 of 1% per month — 8% per year — up to age 70, after which there is no further increase. Enter the monthly amount from your Social Security statement (your PIA, in today's dollars) and the table shows the dollar amount you could expect at each claiming age from 62 to 70, plus the percentage of your full benefit that each choice represents.

Everything runs locally in your browser — no account, no Social Security number needed, nothing uploaded. Social Security is a system of averages: your actual benefit depends on your 35 highest earning years, and this tool assumes your statement's PIA is correct. It is a planning aid, not an SSA statement.

⚠️ Disclaimer: Estimates for planning only — not tax or financial advice. Rates and rules change, so verify with your state or a qualified professional before filing or making big decisions.

📅 What is my Full Retirement Age?
Born 1943–1954: 66 · 1955: 66 + 2 months · 1956: 66 + 4 months · 1957: 66 + 6 months · 1958: 66 + 8 months · 1959: 66 + 10 months · 1960 or later: 67. The SSA treats someone born on January 1 as born in the previous year.

⏳ How much do I lose by claiming at 62?
At FRA 66, claiming at 62 cuts your benefit by 25%. At FRA 67 (born 1960+), it is a 30% cut — $1,000 becomes $700 for life, before cost-of-living adjustments.

📈 How much do I gain by waiting until 70?
Each year past FRA adds 8% (2/3% per month). At FRA 66 that means 32% more at 70; at FRA 67 it is 24% more. The higher amount is locked in for life.

⚖️ Is it better to claim early or wait?
Financially, total lifetime benefits “break even” around your early 80s, but the right choice depends on your health, other income, and family history. Spousal and survivor benefits have their own rules — many couples coordinate so the higher earner delays.

💼 Can I work while collecting?
Yes. If you claim before FRA and keep working, the earnings test withholds part of your benefit above an annual limit; once you reach FRA, the withholding stops and your benefit is recomputed upward.

⚠️ Is this an official SSA tool?
No — it is an independent calculator using the published SSA reduction and credit formulas. For your personal numbers, create an account at ssa.gov/myaccount and read your statement.

📐 Quick formulas
Reduction (early) = 5/9% × first 36 months + 5/12% × months after 36
Credit (delayed) = 2/3% per month after FRA, up to age 70 (≈ 8%/year)
Monthly benefit = PIA × (1 − reduction) or PIA × (1 + credit)
FRA by birth year: 1943–54 → 66 · +2 months/year for 1955–59 · 1960+ → 67
